Remote Sensing Laboratories

These laboratory modules are being developed to provide upper-level undergraduate and first-year graduate students with practical experience in remote sensing and image processing for agricultural monitoring. Students will investigate sensor characteristics, spatial and spectral resolution, vegetation indices, reflectance calibration, and image classification. The modules may be offered independently or as a sequence.

Laboratory 1: Comparison of Satellite Spatial Resolutions for Agricultural Monitoring

Objective: Compare satellite imagery at different spatial resolutions and investigate how resolution affects vegetation-index maps and their interpretation.

Activities

  1. Obtain Sentinel-2 surface reflectance imagery (10 m/pixel) and PlanetScope surface reflectance imagery (approximately 3 m/pixel) of the same site and simiar dates.
  2. Identify the red and NIR bands and compare their spectral characteristics. Align the images to a common geographic reference and select the same study areas.
  3. Calculate the NDVI for both datasets:
    \[\mathrm{NDVI}=\frac{\mathrm{NIR}-\mathrm{Red}}{\mathrm{NIR}+\mathrm{Red}}\]
  4. Display both NDVI maps with the same geographic extent and color scale. Examine field boundaries, vegetation patterns, and exposed soil.
  5. For matching ROIs, calculate mean NDVI and standard deviation. Compare the results and explain possible differences.
  6. Discuss the influence of spatial resolution, spectral response, and acquisition date, as well as the trade-offs between freely available and commercial imagery.

Deliverables: Comparable NDVI maps; a table of sensor characteristics and ROI statistics; a short report interpreting differences and limitations.

Tools: Copernicus Browser, Planet Explorer (subject to data access), Python/Google Colab, ENVI, or MATLAB.
Estimated time: One 3-hour session using prepared datasets.

Laboratory 2: Vegetation and Soil Monitoring Using High-Resolution NAIP Imagery

Objective: Use high-resolution NAIP aerial imagery to distinguish vegetation and exposed soil and compare its capabilities with satellite imagery.

Activities

  1. Obtain NAIP imagery of agricultural fields near the SunGro facility in Mesilla Park. Record the acquisition date, pixel size, and available bands.
  2. Inspect the imagery and locate crops, trees, exposed soil, irrigation structures, and field boundaries. Compare visible detail with Sentinel-2 imagery.
  3. For four-band NAIP imagery with a near-infrared band, calculate NDVI. For RGB-only imagery, calculate an appropriate visible-band index such as Excess Green (ExG):
    \[\mathrm{ExG}=2G-R-B\]
    Use appropriately scaled RGB values and document the scaling.
  4. Create vegetation-index maps and identify representative vegetation and soil ROIs. Calculate summary statistics.
  5. Compare NAIP and Sentinel-2 maps over the same area, noting that different acquisition dates and spectral bands can affect the comparison.
  6. Explain the benefits and limitations of high-resolution aerial imagery for mapping crop rows, individual trees, field edges, and mixed vegetation–soil pixels.

Deliverables: NAIP vegetation-index map; comparison with satellite imagery; ROI statistics table; short interpretation report.

Tools: USGS EarthExplorer or another NAIP data portal; Python/Google Colab, ENVI, or MATLAB.
Estimated time: One 3-hour session using prepared datasets.

Laboratory 3: Reflectance Calibration and Spectral Analysis of Hyperspectral Imagery

Objective: Convert hyperspectral scene measurements into reflectance and investigate spectral differences between vegetation and soil.

Activities

  1. Obtain the hyperspectral scene cube and associated white-reference and dark-reference measurements collected for the Mesilla Park project, when made available in the project repository.
  2. Inspect cube dimensions, wavelength range, number of bands, and image content. Display selected bands to locate vegetation and soil.
  3. Calculate a reflectance cube using compatible scene and reference measurements:
    \[R(x,y,\lambda)=\frac{I(x,y,\lambda)-D(x,y,\lambda)}{W(x,y,\lambda)-D(x,y,\lambda)}R_{\mathrm{ref}}(\lambda)\]
    Here, \(I\) is the scene measurement, \(D\) is the dark reference, \(W\) is the white reference, and \(R_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is the known reference-target reflectance. Avoid division by zero and flag invalid bands or pixels.
  4. Select representative pixels or ROIs from vegetation and exposed soil. Plot reflectance against wavelength.
  5. Identify wavelengths that help distinguish vegetation and soil and explain the observed differences.
  6. Calculate an index supported by the sensor's spectral coverage: NDVI only if NIR and red wavelengths are available; otherwise use an appropriate visible-band measure.
  7. Discuss the importance of reference measurements, calibration, and spectral resolution.

Deliverables: Calibrated reflectance cube; vegetation and soil reflectance plots; selected band or index maps; short calibration and interpretation report.

Tools: Scene, white-reference, and dark-reference hyperspectral datasets; Python/Google Colab, ENVI, or MATLAB.
Estimated time: One 3-hour session using prepared datasets.

Laboratory 4: Classification of Multispectral and Hyperspectral Imagery for Agricultural Monitoring

Objective: Apply unsupervised classification to agricultural imagery and investigate the effects of spectral inputs, spatial resolution, and number of clusters.

Activities

  1. Obtain prepared datasets from Sentinel-2 (10 m/pixel for selected bands) or PlanetScope (approximately 3 m/pixel), NAIP aerial imagery, and the project hyperspectral observations. Record the bands and spatial resolution available for each.
  2. Examine the images and select relevant spectral bands or vegetation indices for separating vegetation and exposed soil.
  3. Calculate appropriate vegetation indices, such as NDVI when red and NIR are present or ExG for RGB imagery.
  4. Apply K-means clustering to selected bands and/or indices, starting with three clusters. Scale features appropriately before clustering.
  5. Repeat with different cluster counts, such as 3, 5, and 7. Compare the resulting maps and the interpretability of the clusters.
  6. Examine original imagery and cluster spectral characteristics before assigning descriptive labels. Note that K-means cluster numbers do not automatically correspond to physical land-cover classes.
  7. Compare classification results across the available datasets and discuss differences caused by spatial resolution and spectral content.
  8. Where reference observations exist, compare the clusters with known vegetation and soil areas; otherwise describe the evaluation as qualitative.

Deliverables: Vegetation-index and K-means maps; table of input features and cluster settings; short report on interpretation and limitations.

Tools: Python/Google Colab, ENVI, or MATLAB.
Estimated time: One 3-hour session using prepared datasets.
